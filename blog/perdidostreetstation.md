# Review: Perdido Street Station

I want to talk about one of my favourite speculative fiction experiences of this year: China Miéville's scintillating, intoxicating, *sickening* turn-of-the-millennium novel *Perdido Street Station* — one of those rare fantasy epics that mostly justifies it's blisteringly long page count. Perdido Street Station sits prettily at 867 pages, which for any other genre would be considered farcically long (that's 70% of the way to *War and Peace*!) but in modern fantasy is pretty standard fare. Although I'm happy to argue for hours on end that most fantasy books shouldn't be as long as they are, that's a discussion for another day. *Perdido Street Station* is a book that has me asking more fundamental questions, like: *do I have any business calling this a fantasy book at all?*

*Perdido Street Station* seems to insist that it's not. From the first, this book oozes with the desire to escape it's genre label, slippery like the lubricated tendrils of a slake-moth. My notes for this review are tagged with no less than seven different genre labels. If nothing else, this book is imaginative; you might think that this relentless commitment to genre-hopping would end up being aesthetically muddled, but instead, *Perdido Street*'s kaleidoscopic absorption of every kind of speculative fiction you can think of lands us in a place that's both coherent and *entirely unique*. (I'm unconvinced that steampunk science-fantasy surreal weird dystopian horror is a genre that's dying to take off, though.)

Miéville's writing seems to have merited hundreds of comparisons to other authors over the years, from Kafka to Lewis Carroll, but to me there's one clear point of comparison: Terry Pratchett, for this is a novel that is at once beguilingly funny and deeply critical of contemporary British society. Miéville certainly belongs to a peculiar tradition of British fantasy that he has made entirely his own.

By the time that I was done with this book though, I couldn't help but feel a little disappointed.

At the heart of the plot is Miéville's invented city of New Crobuzon, which is both a love letter to London and a stark reflection of it's social inequities. His introduction, which showcases Miéville's immaculate descriptive prose, is nothing less than a masterclass in introducing your setting to a reader, given to us through the eyes of Yagharek: one of the book's three POV characters, and a member of a species of anarchistic bird-people ("Garuda") with an inscrutable code of conduct. 

Miéville's penchant for designing unique and fascinating fantasy species doesn't stop there: shortly after, we are introduced to a species of sentient cacti ("Cactacae"), a matriarchal society of scarab-headed humanoids ("Khepri"), and a handful of slippery water spirits from Slavic mythology ("Vodyanoi"). These social groups will become formative to the development of the plot, and together provide the impression of a vibrant multicultural society. All of this, of course is underscored by a brutally capitalistic industrial economy, overseen by an ancient government enmired in corruption — one which punishes it's disobedient citizens by "Remaking" them into creatures meant to invite disgust and revulsion from their peers. 

Needless to say, all of this is excellent — if there's one thing about Miéville's novel that you can take for granted, it's that the depth of his worldbuilding and the social critiques it contains are guaranteed to have you enraptured, and the more surreal and horrifying elements that build as the novel continues maintain this standard. Some of his descriptions are absolutely standout, swaddled in evocative metaphor, and flowing like the vast and polluted rivers Tar and Canker.

The plot kicks off with Isaac, our scientist protagonist, and his Khepri lover, an artist named Lin, and we are invited to explore some of the social and economic tensions which underscore their lives. Shortly after, Isaac meets Yagharek, who tasks him with the replacement of his lost wings — taken from him as the punishment for a shameful crime he committed against his people, a "choice-theft of the second degree." Meanwhile, Lin is tasked with a commission from a mysterious benefactor: a sculpture made from Khepri spit, an artistic medium specific to her people.

To rapidly summarise an 800-page plot with minimal spoilers, Isaac ends up releasing something terrible during the course of his research, and the three of them (as well as their handful of friends) end up careening about the city on a handful of heists and investigations, navigating the complex and disparate web of factions in New Crobuzon's underbelly whilst trying to avoid the authorities. Along the way, we are introduced to even more incredible and fantastical inhabitants of the city: the Weavers, the slake-moths, and the Construct Council, which examine the nature of consciousness and artistic creativity in ways that I shall not spoil here. 

Flaws in Miéville's writing style began to reveal themselves to me as I progressed. He has a habit of using an overly sophisticated word where a simpler one could do (any writer that finds themself indiscriminately replacing "dark" with "stygian" should really examine this habit), which he seems to have gotten from Lovecraft. Reading *Perdido Street* I've encountered the words "desultory," "etiolate," and "skeins" more times than ever in my life prior. *Perdido Street* has certainly taught me plenty of new words, from "femtoscopic" to "desquamate" to "obstreperous," but I think this tendency makes an already-inaccessible 900-page book even less accessible with needlessly complicated vocabulary that doesn't actually communicate anything not communicated by the words "noisy" or "fast." Contrary to popular writing advice, doing everything you can to avoid using simple words like "said" doesn't make for a better novel: it makes for a slog. That said, I never found these details so distracting as to overpower the imaginative worldbuilding and good-enough plotting.

Although I so highly praised his descriptive writing earlier, as the book progressed I felt like it was deployed inexpertly at times. When we're first introduced to a new creature (the slake-moths, for example), an elaborate description might be highly engaging; but when that creature has appeared five times already, that description just ends up padding out the page count. I felt this especially when it came to *Perdido Street*'s action scenes — what could be a shorter chapter ends up needlessly extended with repetitive description and sluggish pacing. Much of this comes down to personal preference — I prefer my fantasy books shorter and with less blundering description, whereas some folks, especially those with stronger visual imaginations than mine, prefer a more cinematic approach where the prose gives a more complete picture. For me, Le Guin's plain but heartful prose will always remain the gold standard.

Although *Perdido Street* remains a compulsively readable book, there's one place where those pages of description feel like they would have been more deserved, and that is *character*. Isaac has interesting relationships with Lin and Yagharek, but Lin is absent for much of the second half of the book, and there's shockingly little character interaction between Isaac, Yag, and the remaining "party members." One particular disappointment is the introduction of three new adventurers for the climax of the book, who are treated as little more than plot devices to facilitate Isaac and Yag's plans. Each of these characters comes from a different culture and socioeconomic background, but their interior worlds are barely examined at all, which makes for flat, two-dimensional characterisation and zero internal conflict. 

For a novel which aims to explore political interactions across classes and species, it's a shame that Miéville displays aversion to discussing his main characters' relationships. Part of this may come from Miéville's commitment to Marxist materialism, which emphasises larger-scale class conflict rather than personal squabbles, but Miéville seems resistant to the feminist and intersectional idea that *the personal is also political*.

Which brings me to the shortcoming of this book which I find the most disappointing. After we are introduced to Lin, a compelling character at first who would have made an excellent protagonist, she is completely absent from the second half of the book until the climax, where Miéville uses her to reproduce a classic damsel-in-distress trope where Isaac rescues her from the novel's antagonists. At no point in this sequence do we see from Lin's perspective, and at no point does the narrative scrutinise this trope. For a writer that tried so hard to distance himself from the socially conservative fantasies of The Lord of the Rings and it's imitators, this is a shockingly misogynistic and regressive way to treat the female lead. Another plot point revealed at the end of the novel, relating to our main character Yagharek, is similarly misogynistic in its execution. As a result of these elements, I can't help but have more complicated feelings about a novel that I could have straightforwardly loved. For all that this book is ahead of it's time in so many ways, I am reminded of the extent to which such starkly misogynistic themes proliferated in fantasy as recently as 26 years ago, even for a progressive author, and continue to proliferate in fantasy writing today.

Although I was plenty disappointed with the way that the events of *Perdido Street Station* played out, I remain dazzled by it's brilliant and innovative worldbuilding and approach to fantasy writing overall. This review might feel unfairly negative for all the praise I've given, but trust me when I say that Miéville's writing is really spectacular, and is best left to speak for itself — as much as I have plenty to complain about, I was still an avid consumer. In short, I'll be excited to return to Miéville's writing and the city of New Crobuzon sometime soon. Despite this novel's shortcomings, it should be considered essential reading for any fan of postmodern fantasy, and will certainly dazzle you as it did me. So long!
