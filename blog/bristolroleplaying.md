# Bristol's Roleplaying Heritage


I was poring over my recently acquired copy of *The Elusive Shift* when something caught my eye. Bristol, the city that I study in and consider my home, was actually the site of some of the critical developments that transformed skirmish wargames (in which a player controls units that represent just a single character) into the very first role playing games. This article is about the parts of that history that I take pride in, and the parts of it that I'm glad we're moving on from.

In 1969 and 1970, Steve Curtis, Ian Colwill and Mike Blake, a group of Bristolian wargamers, developed a series of rules for simulating skirmish wargaming in the context of the American Old West, which was published under the name of *Western Gunfight* (1970). Unlike many other games at the time, *Western Gunfight* players personalise their characters, giving them a name, attributes, personality traits, and the like. This was an enormous step forward in the shift between skirmish wargames and TTRPGs. When a wargame transitions between players gaming as a unit of many soldiers to gaming as a single character, players very quickly begin to think about who this character is, and might even take the step of acting in a manner that is "in character," even if it's not the most strategically viable. On the personal scale, where the rigid military discipline of large units has broken down, you might begin to empathise with the tiny, revolver-toting figurine in front of you that just got *unfortunately* bested in a shootout outside the saloon. 

Curtis, Colwill and Blake eagerly published the results of their experimentation in Donald Featherstone's widely read zine, *Wargamer's Newsletter*. They organised campaigns, in which their recurring characters get into various rootin' tootin' scenarios, and between games played out character interactions that took place in the local saloon. Via *Wargamer's Newsletter*, these developments were eagerly followed by Gary Gygax up in Wisconsin, who was without a doubt inspired by these rules in the process of developing the very first edition of Dungeons & Dragons. Interestingly, the Bristol wargamers were also some of the very first wargamers to adopt the use of a 20-sided dice, which would grow to become a keystone of D&D and role-playing titles like it.

I was delighted when I found out that this city I live in had such a formative impact on the early history of my own hobby. In our culture's eagerness to attribute collective works to a single person, I had assumed that Gary Gygax was the sole contributor to the shift between wargaming and roleplaying, and I now realise that this was a ridiculous idea. The early history of roleplaying (well-documented in Jon Peterson's *The Elusive Shift*) is so much more collaborative than I had realised, and there's really no way to say whether it is truly *Dungeons & Dragons* that can be called the very first roleplaying game or something else. I'm proud to live in a city which contributed to that cultural shift. rawr

If you're just interested in the history of wargaming and roleplaying, that's all I have to say. But there's something else which I uncovered in myself while I was researching for this article, and that is a discomfort with the origins of my favourite pastime that I didn't realise I had. While scanning through old editions of the *Wargamer's Newsletter*, I began to feel uncomfortable with the way that this subculture talked about war and the gruesome machinery of it. I became cognizant of the fact that wargaming was an overwhelmingly white and male hobby. I became aware of the strands of this culture that are interwoven with the history of role-playing, and the strands that continue to affect the trajectory of role-playing today.

I don't want to relitigate the ethical issues of a magazine that's so old it's sold in pounds and shillings. However, there still remains a culture amongst players of D&D and other games today that takes the worst, most jingoistic aspects of Western culture and plays them out on a miniature grid. That excludes women, people of colour and queer people from it's table, and does what they can to prevent themselves from mixing with a diverse group of gamers. D&D, in particular, enables a particular power fantasy to be realised that is white, male, colonial and violently patriarchal. D&D is inexorably tied up with that power fantasy, and as long as it dominates our tabletop role-playing scene, in some way that will continue to be the legacy of tabletop roleplaying.

I don't really have an answer to this. It's just an expression of my discomfort with D&D's history. But I do think there's a need to separate this hobby from it's roots in racist power fantasies, both by breaking the monopoly that D&D has over tabletop role-playing and by continuing to take D&D in a different direction. There's so much creativity in the indie roleplaying scene now that mainstream gamers really should tap into. Like the gamers of the 1970s, like Curtis, Colwill  and Blake, we should all be thinking about roleplaying again, and rethink the stories that we tell around the table from the very beginning. That's where I'm hoping that the future of roleplaying games goes.
